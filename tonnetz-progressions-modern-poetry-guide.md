## The invitation: harmony as a path, not a verdict

Imagine a poem whose speaker walks through a familiar room after everyone else has left. The furniture is recognizable; the distances between things are not. A functional tonal progression can narrate arrival and departure with considerable force. But what if the room keeps changing just enough that “home” never quite settles? A chain of nearby triads can create that experience: each chord feels connected to the last, while the expected destination stays just beyond reach.

The Tonnetz offers one way to compose such connections. The word means “tone network”: in its familiar neo-Riemannian use, it is a lattice in which pitch classes are arranged by intervallic relations, making certain transformations among major and minor triads visually and conceptually legible. It is less a map of where music must go than a map of which nearby doors are open. Richard Cohn's account of neo-Riemannian operations describes how triads can relate through parsimonious voice leading—often by retaining two tones and moving the third by a semitone or whole step.[1] David Lewin's transformational approach, and later work by Dmitri Tymoczko, broaden the question from “What chord is this?” to “What musical action connects these sonorities?”[2]

That shift is useful for poetry. A chord need not behave like a label attached to a word (“minor means sadness,” etc.). Instead, a progression can act like syntax: a turn, an echo, a refusal, a delayed answer. A pitch held across two chords can resemble a word that changes meaning in context; a small chromatic shift can register as a change of light rather than a change of address. The listener hears continuity and difference at once.

The rhythmic half of the proposal needs a similar change of perspective. “Neo-Gothic rhythm” is not a standard analytic category with one accepted definition. Here it is a composer's working term for a family of choices that may evoke the uncanny or the ritualistic: a pulse that persists while groupings shift; an ostinato that is almost, but not quite, predictable; weighty silences; accents that arrive a beat early; or a repeated pattern stretched until repetition feels like suspense. These devices are not inherently Gothic. They become part of a Gothic or neo-Gothic aesthetic through context, performance, timbre, text, and the listener's cultural associations. Isabella van Elferen's *Gothic Music: The Sounds of the Uncanny* is useful background for thinking about Gothic music as an aesthetic and cultural field, rather than reducing it to a minor chord plus fog machine.[3]

The central craft question, then, is not “How do I make this sound spooky?” It is: **What does this poem make time and harmonic expectation do?**

## A small map of the Tonnetz

Take C major: C–E–G. Its nearest neo-Riemannian neighbors include:

- **L (Leading-tone exchange):** C major becomes E minor, retaining E and G while C moves down by semitone to B.
- **R (Relative):** C major becomes A minor, retaining C and E while G moves to A.
- **P (Parallel):** C major becomes C minor, retaining C and G while E moves down by semitone to E-flat.

The labels are conventional names for transformations, not mood instructions. They can be applied in either direction, and their sound depends on register, spacing, instrumentation, and what came before. Connect these moves and a composer can make progressions that travel a long way while each step changes very little. For instance:

**C major —L→ E minor —R→ G major —L→ B minor —R→ D major**

This sequence keeps moving its root upward by thirds, while adjacent triads share two pitch classes. It offers a coherent, chromatic-mediant-like route without using a conventional dominant-to-tonic cadence as its engine. One could arrange the chords so common tones remain in the same register, or deliberately break that smoothness with a leap, a registral gap, or a sudden change in orchestration.

This is where theoretical precision matters. “Parsimonious” means voice leading with small total motion in a particular representation; it does not mean the passage is automatically easy to sing, emotionally subtle, or free of tonal implication. Nor does a Tonnetz replace a full account of meter, phrase, bass motion, or tonal hearing. Cohn's *Audacious Euphony* examines the expressive and historical reach of triadic chromaticism, while Tymoczko's *A Geometry of Music* gives a broader geometric account of voice leading and musical space.[4] These approaches are powerful lenses, not universal explanations.

Try hearing a Tonnetz progression in at least three ways:

1. **As a route:** Which triads are neighbors, and which paths make a satisfying or surprising journey?
2. **As voice leading:** Which notes stay, which move by semitone, and which jump? Does the bass tell a different story from the upper voices?
3. **As tonal rhetoric:** What does the passage make you expect? Does it confirm a center, evade one, or encourage several plausible centers?

The third question guards against a common analytical trap: a visual model can make a path look inevitable when, to a listener, it might sound aimless—or clearly tonal in a way the diagram does not explain. Geometry is a good sketchbook. It is not a substitute for listening.

## A pulse that remembers—and misremembers

If Tonnetz harmony offers nearby doors, rhythm can determine whether the listener walks, hesitates, circles, or hears footsteps coming from a corridor that should be empty.

For this article, a neo-Gothic rhythmic vocabulary might combine four ingredients:

- **A recognizable pulse** (a slow beat, a steady eighth-note tread, or an implied dance);
- **An unstable surface** (uneven groupings such as 2+3 or 3+2+2, shifting accents, or cross-rhythm);
- **Repetition with a crack in it** (an ostinato that changes one duration, note, or accent each return);
- **Suspended time** (rests, fermatas, long decays, or a cadence withheld past the point of comfort).

The idea is not to pile on “odd meters” as shorthand for darkness. A bar of 7/8 can sound like a brisk folk dance; a perfectly regular 4/4 can become uncanny if the phrase ending keeps arriving somewhere else. Meter is heard through patterned emphasis and temporal expectation, not just the time signature printed above the staff. Justin London's work on musical meter and Harald Krebs's analysis of metrical dissonance offer rigorous tools for understanding pulse, grouping, and conflict.[5] They do not prescribe a Gothic effect; they help describe the rhythmic mechanisms a composer might put to aesthetic use.

A practical approach is to establish a steady pulse before disturbing it. Let a low instrument play quarter notes at a moderate tempo. Above it, group attacks as 3+3+2 eighth notes. After several repetitions, shift the upper pattern by one eighth note while the bass stays put. The listener can now retain the old pulse while recalibrating the surface. If the pattern also changes harmony at irregular intervals, the music may feel less like “a meter in 8/8” and more like a ritual whose rules are being revised mid-ceremony.

Silence deserves equal status. In a text setting, a rest after an enjambed line can keep the sentence moving in the listener's mind while the instruments stop. A breath can delay a word that the syntax has already promised. This is not empty time; it is a form of musical punctuation. The composer should decide whether the silence belongs to the speaker, the listener, or the world around them.

## The poem is not a libretto-shaped metronome

Modern poetry is not one style. Free verse, experimental lineation, documentary text, surreal imagery, spoken language, and inherited forms each offer different rhythmic evidence. A poem's line break is not necessarily a breath mark; punctuation does not dictate tempo; and an apparent stress pattern may change when spoken by a particular performer. Begin with a voice, not an abstract scansion.

Read the poem aloud several times, first for meaning and then for sound. Mark:

- lexical stress and unstressed syllables;
- pauses that feel syntactic versus pauses that feel emotional;
- enjambments that pull the thought forward;
- repeated words, consonants, vowels, or images;
- places where the speaker revises, contradicts, or withholds.

Now compare those events with the music's formal landmarks. A Tonnetz transformation can underline a shift in perspective without making a chord-change happen on every important noun. A repeating rhythmic cell can suggest a recurring image, then fracture when the poem's speaker discovers that recurrence is not the same as safety. A sustained pitch can bridge two harmonies while a line break interrupts the voice. Sometimes the most faithful response to a poem is not to set every syllable clearly; sometimes intelligibility is the point. Choose, and make the choice audible.

Avoid the tempting equation “dark text = minor chords.” A poem's darkness may come from an image that is lucid rather than vague, from a mundane object made strange, or from a speaker who refuses the expected emotional climax. A constant minor sonority can flatten those distinctions. Likewise, chromatic harmony does not automatically make a text modern, and irregular meter does not automatically make it expressive. Technique can clarify the poem, complicate it, or compete with it; the composer has to hear which is happening.

Ethics also matter when setting living poets or archival speech. Ask permission where appropriate, follow publication and performance rights, credit translators and source-makers, and preserve context when adapting testimony. Documentary language is not raw material without a history. Julia Wolfe's *Anthracite Fields*, for example, draws on varied historical sources and voices connected to Pennsylvania's anthracite coal region; its texts are not simply interchangeable “poetry.” The composer's project and the provenance of the words belong in how we listen.[6]

## Three listening rooms

These works do not all use the same harmonic language or claim to exemplify “neo-Gothic Tonnetz composition.” They are listening companions: each offers a distinct way to hear text, time, repetition, or atmosphere.

### George Crumb — *Apparition: Elegiac Songs and Vocalises* (1979)

Crumb sets texts by Walt Whitman for soprano and amplified piano. Whitman is a nineteenth-century poet, not a contemporary poet; the relevance here is his modern-feeling free-verse scale, open syntax, and treatment of death and mourning. The cycle's vocalises and unusual piano sonorities make instrumental sound part of the poem's atmosphere rather than mere accompaniment. Listen for how recurrence and timbral change alter the meaning of a return. Do not assume that every sonority is a Tonnetz transformation: the work is a model for text, resonance, and formal imagination, not evidence that a specific theory generated it.

- [Complete performance of *Apparition*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nUswswn65SY)
- [Work information and score listing from Wise Music Classical](https://www.wisemusicclassical.com/work/69496/)

### Arnold Schoenberg — *Pierrot lunaire*, Op. 21 (1912)

Schoenberg's melodrama sets twenty-one poems by Albert Giraud, in Otto Erich Hartleben's German translation. Its Sprechstimme—between speaking and singing—makes vocal delivery itself a compositional question. The work is a useful counterweight to the idea that poetry must be regularized into a song meter: register, articulation, instrumental color, and shifting character can carry formal weight even when the vocal line resists conventional melody. Listen for recurring figures and contrasts, but remember that the poem's translation is part of the work's history.

- [Chicago Symphony Orchestra performance, *Complete performance: Schoenberg's Pierrot lunaire*](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=bd2cBUJmDr8)

### Julia Wolfe — *Anthracite Fields* (2014)

Wolfe's Pulitzer Prize-winning oratorio combines choir and chamber ensemble with text drawn from historical and documentary materials about coal mining and the people of Pennsylvania's anthracite region. Its repeated patterns and forceful ensemble writing provide a compelling study in how musical insistence can carry collective memory. Listen for the relationship between the material's particular sources and the work's large-scale musical gestures. Treat it as a model of research-led text setting, not as a generic “dark industrial” effect. Wolfe's site gives context for the work and its libretto; PBS has a performance excerpt.

- [PBS performance excerpt and program description](https://www.pbs.org/video/anthracite-fields-julia-wolfe-tqvfna/)
- [Julia Wolfe's work page and libretto materials](https://juliawolfemusic.com/music/anthracite-fields/)

One useful comparison across the three: Crumb lets timbre and resonance inhabit an elegiac text; Schoenberg makes vocal gesture unstable and theatrical; Wolfe makes repetition and ensemble force answer to historical voices. None is a recipe. Each shows that the relation between musical system and text is a choice with consequences.

## A composer's workshop: five exercises

### 1. Make a four-chord Tonnetz walk

Choose a major or minor triad. Write down three neighboring triads connected by P, L, or R. Keep one common tone in the same register wherever practical. Sing each voice separately, then play the progression as block chords.

**Variation:** Keep the same chord sequence, but change the inversion and register. Note how a “smooth” transformation can become angular when the bass leaps or the common tone moves to a different octave.

**Question:** What did the lattice predict accurately, and what did it leave out?

### 2. Let line breaks control the pulse

Choose a short public-domain poem or a text you have permission to use. Speak it naturally and record yourself. Tap the pulse you hear without forcing a time signature. Transcribe only the attacks, pauses, and stressed syllables—not pitches.

Set the passage once with line endings as rests, then again with enjambments carried across barlines. Compare which version makes the speaker sound more certain, more hesitant, or more ironic. Neither version is automatically “correct”; explain what each one does.

### 3. Build a ritual that changes one thing

Create a one-bar ostinato in 5/8 or 7/8. Repeat it eight times. On each return, alter just one parameter: duration, articulation, dynamic, pitch, or accent. Keep the pulse steady, then try shifting the ostinato against the pulse by one subdivision.

Record both versions. If the second sounds merely busier, undo a layer. Unease usually needs legibility before it can unsettle.

### 4. Set a line with harmonic restraint

Take one poetic sentence with a meaningful turn or contradiction. Set it over a single triad or drone. Let rhythm, register, articulation, and silence do the work. Then add only one Tonnetz transformation at the sentence's turn.

**Compare:** Does the chord change reveal the turn, overexplain it, or pull attention away from the words?

### 5. Compose the afterimage

Write a short passage in which the voice stops before the music does. Let one pitch, chord tone, or rhythmic cell continue through the silence. On its final return, change one note by semitone or shift its accent by one subdivision.

Ask a listener what they thought was continuing: the speaker, the memory, or the accompaniment. You need not control the answer completely, but you should know what evidence in the score invites it.

For every exercise, make two quick realizations: one sparse and one saturated. Then remove anything that explains what the poem has already made clear. Gothic atmosphere is not measured in the number of low notes, reverberation seconds, or ominous tritones per minute. Even the fog machine appreciates a little restraint.

## A wise way to use the theory

Use the Tonnetz when you want to explore smooth triadic connection, chromatic voice leading, or routes that do not depend on conventional functional syntax. Then check what tonal hearing, bass motion, meter, and performance contribute beyond the diagram. Use an intentionally “neo-Gothic” rhythmic vocabulary when repetition, temporal uncertainty, or suspended expectation feels relevant to the poem—not because the label promises instant atmosphere. And let the poem resist you. Its breaths, syntax, images, and silences may require the music to abandon the most elegant progression in the room.

The best result is not a fusion in which harmony, rhythm, and text all say the same thing at once. It may be a carefully managed disagreement: a pulse that insists while the harmony wanders; a voice that moves forward while the instruments remember; a cadence that arrives beneath a line whose meaning remains unresolved. That is a kind of wonder worth composing—not because it is obscure, but because the listener can feel the question being asked.
